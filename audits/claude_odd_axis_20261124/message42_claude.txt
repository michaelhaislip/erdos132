In both cases the question becomes when a sum of three roots of unity of odd order is real.

**Lemma 2 (real sum of three roots of odd order).** Let a, b, c be roots of unity whose orders divide an odd M. Then a+b+c is real exactly when one of these holds:
- (i) the multiset {a,b,c} equals its inverse, which (because 1 is the only element of odd order equal to its own inverse) means {a,b,c} = {1, d, d⁻¹};
- (ii) {a,b,c} = η·{1, ω, ω²}, where ω is a primitive cube root of unity.

*Proof.* Write the relation a+b+c−ā−b̄−c̄ = 0 with terms εη, where ε = ±1 and η has odd order. That gives three terms with ε = + and three with ε = −. Split the sum into minimal vanishing sub-collections.
- **Length 2:** the two terms are ζ and −ζ. Since −1 has even order, they have the same η and opposite signs.
- **Length 3:** the terms form an equilateral triangle ζ(1+ω+ω²). The ratios have odd order, so all three terms share one sign.
- **Length 4:** never minimal. By plane geometry, four unit vectors summing to zero pair off into opposite pairs.
- **Length 5:** impossible, because it would leave one term to vanish alone.
- **Length 6, minimal:** excluded, as follows. If the same η appears with both signs, those two terms cancel and the sum is not minimal, so the coefficients satisfy Σ|c| = 6 and split 3/3 by sign. At most 6 distinct η occur, so Mann's theorem puts every ratio in μ₃₀; the ratios have odd order, so they lie in μ₁₅. Normalise each η to ω^i·ζ₅^j and write the coefficients as a 3×5 array c_{ij}. Using 1+ω+ω² = 0 and the fact that 1, ω is a basis of Q(ζ₁₅) over Q(ζ₅), the vanishing forces c_{ij} = g_i + f_j.
  - If g is constant, the support is a union of whole columns, each a coset of μ₃ with one sign. Two columns give a proper vanishing subsum. One column has a single sign, which contradicts the 3/3 split.
  - If f is constant, the number of nonzero entries is a multiple of 5 and at most 6, so one row of 5 carries total weight 6, which is impossible.
  - Otherwise you need at least 9 zero entries, and the number of zeros is Σ m_v·n_{−v}, with column multiplicities m_v ≤ 4 and row multiplicities n_w ≤ 2. The maximum is 4·2 + 1·1 = 9, and reaching it forces the pattern R5:R3: four entries of one sign and two of the other. That contradicts the 3/3 split.

So the blocks are either {3,3}, where each block has one sign, giving case (ii), or {2,2,2}, where the + multiset equals the − multiset, giving case (i). ∎

**Theorem (whole split regime, odd ambient field).** With x and z of odd order and z ≠ 1, the solutions of (T) are exactly these:

| s | block type | solutions | family |
|---|---|---|---|
| +1 | {2,2,2} | z = x⁻¹ with u = 1 or u = x | F1 |
| +1 | {3,3} | z = x² with u = ω^{±1}·x⁻¹ | F2 |
| −1 | {2,2,2} | z = x with u = −1 or u = −x⁻¹ | F1 |
| −1 | {3,3} | z = x⁻² with u = −ω^{±1}·x | F2 |

The s = +1 rows come from placing the 1 of {1, d, d⁻¹} at wz or at w⁻¹, or from the coset condition. The s = −1 rows come the same way.

- **No x=1 solutions:** placing the 1 at x forces z = 1 in every case. Directly, x = 1 turns (T) into u² = −z⁻¹. That is impossible when u = ±w, because the left side then has odd order and the right side even order. So any x=1 family needs i, meaning a conductor divisible by 4, which is outside the odd ambient field.
- **No other exceptions:** every (x,z) outside F1 and F2 is excluded in the split regime.

**Remaining obstacle: the non-split regime.** This is when z has prime-power order p^k and −1 is not in the decomposition group D_p. Here π/(1−z) is not integral, so Kronecker's theorem does not apply. This plan is unchecked:
1. For σ in D_p, σ(π)/π is a unit of absolute value 1 at every embedding, so it is a root of unity ζ_σ.
2. Combine σβ − σx = ζ(β − x) with its complex conjugate. Since β and σβ are real, when ζ ≠ ±1 this gives β(ζ − ζ⁻¹) = σx⁻¹ − σx + ζx − ζ⁻¹x⁻¹.
